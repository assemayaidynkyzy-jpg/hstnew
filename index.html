Section 0 – Introduction

Author’s thesis – central argument

The artisans and small traders of Istanbul (esnaf) helped start the 1730 rebellion, then changed sides and backed the new sultan by March 1731.

Olson reads this switch against Şerif Mardin’s “center vs. periphery” model of Ottoman politics.

a fundamental change in the alignment of the ‘center’ OLSON, P. 341
Olson (1974), p. 341

Section 0 – Introduction

Three views of 1730

Karal – a new mentality stopped.The Tulip Period was the first serious attempt at Western-style reform. The 1730 revolt ended it, and later reforming sultans risked being deposed.
Berkes – a religious reaction.Opposition to reform took a religious colour: reformers were blamed for harming both religion and state, and for dealings with foreign powers.
Mardin – center against periphery.1730 was the first revolt to show a pattern that repeated: a Westernising elite, and the ulema using the masses against it.

Olson’s question: how did the esnaf add to the masses’ alienation from their rulers?

Olson (1974), pp. 329–331
Section 0 – Introduction

Center and periphery (Mardin)

The center
  • The Sultanate
  • The ulema (learned religious class)
  • The military elite (askeri): in 1730 the Janissaries and the Sultan’s ministers
The periphery
  • Peasants (reaya) and provincial notables
  • Non-Muslim communities (millets)
  • The lower classes of the city and the esnaf, the “petite bourgeoisie” of artisans and merchants

Mardin’s claim: before the nineteenth century the main confrontation was one-dimensional, always the center against the periphery.

Olson (1974), pp. 330–331, 341–342
Section 1 – Why the esnaf rebelled

Istanbul under strain

After the treaties of 1699–1700 the Ottomans turned east, and in 1723 the Persian front reopened.

  • Higher taxes, rising food prices, shortages.
  • Refugees from the east and migrants from Rumeli flowed into the capital.
  • The city had to feed them and find them work.
Olson (1974), p. 333
Section 1 – Why the esnaf rebelled

Three main complaints

The currency.Debasement of the akçe, a silver shortage and counterfeit coins hurt trade and the food supply.
The guilds themselves.Migrants, non-guild producers, new middlemen (dellals) and Janissaries in trade put the guild system under strain.
The ordu akçesi.An extra campaign tax for the Persian war; the army then waited at Üsküdar and the esnaf felt cheated.

Then news arrived that Nadir Shah had taken Tabriz, and the unrest broke out (September 1730).

Olson (1974), pp. 333–339
Section 1 – Why the esnaf rebelled

The ordu akçesi, 1730

27esnaf
84tents (haymes)
3,168,800akçe paid
  • The tax bought the right to sell goods to the army; each guild paid per tent, and the 1730 rate was much higher than before.
  • The army waited at Üsküdar, and rumours said much of the money had been embezzled.
  • Rumour made Patrona Halil a small secondhand-goods dealer who had hoped to sell old clothes to the army.
Olson (1974), pp. 337–339
Section 2 – The rebellion

The rebellion, 1730

  • Esnaf leaders were at the front of the rebellion (September 1730).
  • The rebels obtained the execution of Grand Vizier Ibrahim Paşa.
  • Ahmed III was deposed on 2 October 1730; Mahmud I became sultan.
  • Mahmud I promised to cancel the extra campaign taxes.
  • Patrona Halil was executed on 25 November 1730.
Olson (1974), p. 339
Section 2 – The rebellion

March 1731: the rebellion again

  • The revolt of 25 March 1731 came four months after Patrona Halil’s execution.
  • Trigger: the execution of the Greek butcher Kasab Yanak, whom Patrona had named governor (Hospodar) of Moldavia.
  • Mahmud I displayed the sacred banner (Sancak-ı Şerif), usually used against foreign enemies, to quell it.
  • Topal Osman Paşa, who helped suppress it, was rewarded with the Grand Vizierate.
  • There were further rebellions, with arson, on 21 July and 2 September 1731.
Olson (1974), p. 340
Section 3 – The realignment

The esnaf change sides

A British report (Lord Kinnoull, April 1731) says the failed rebels made the Sultan stronger: the city’s merchants and tradesmen would back him unless he made himself hated by new impositions.

The promise.Mahmud I promised to cancel the extra campaign taxes.
The war.The reopened Persian front let the esnaf recoup what they had invested in the army guilds.
Tired of revolt.Constant rebellion hurt business and trade.
The government’s signal.In 1732 a new Grand Vizier’s first task was to restore confidence in the currency and revive trade.
Olson (1974), pp. 340–341

Section 3 – The realignment

What changed afterwards

Janissaries weakened.The esnaf could now balance them, which later helped the centralizing policies of Selim III and Mahmud II.
Masses more alienated.Ulema leaders and the masses often opposed a reforming sultan; the esnaf, especially Christians and Jews trading with Europe, backed more contact with the West.
Privileges.The Sultan rewarded esnaf support with more commercial, tax and legal privileges for Christian esnaf.
1740.In that Istanbul revolt Mahmud I armed the esnaf, including Christians and Jews, to help suppress it.
Olson (1974), p. 343
Section 4 – Discussion

Limits Olson admits

Overlapping groups.Many esnaf were enrolled Janissaries, two bodies that sometimes clashed.
Unknown numbers.He cannot tell what share of the esnaf were Muslim, Christian or Jewish.
Who supported the Sultan?Equally Muslims and non-Muslims, or mostly Christians and Jews? Not known.
Support from Baer.Guilds limited to one community were more common in the 1700s, which fits his idea of polarization.
Olson (1974), pp. 342–344
Section 4 – Discussion

Why does this matter?

Economic roots.The revolt grew from guild grievances about money, taxes and the guild order, not only palace politics.
A test of Mardin.Alliances could shift: the same group could oppose a sultan in 1730 and back him in 1731.
A later split.The masses and ulema on one side, a Westernizing court backed by trading minorities on the other.
Olson (1974)
Section 4 – Discussion

Analytical question

Olson’s “realignment” rests on one report by the British resident (April 1731), a few likely reasons for the esnaf’s change of mind, and an admission that he cannot say who in the guilds backed the Sultan.

When the evidence about a whole social group comes mostly from one foreign observer, how far can it carry an argument about a “realignment”, and what other sources (guild records, court registers) would we need before we agree?

Olson (1974), pp. 341–344
